Ions and Charges

Cations (+)

Positively charged ions formed when an atom loses electrons. E.g., Na loses 1e- to become Na+.

Anions (-)

Negatively charged ions formed when an atom gains electrons. E.g., Cl gains 1e- to become Cl-.

Charge = Protons - Electrons. Valence electrons (outermost shell) are most involved in bonding and ion formation.

Periods and Groups

Periods (Horizontal Rows)

Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells. Period number = principal energy level of valence electrons.

Groups (Vertical Columns)

Group 1 (Alkali Metals): 1 valence e-, highly reactive

Group 2 (Alkaline Earth): 2 valence e-

Groups 3-12 (Transition Metals): varied properties, colored compounds

Group 17 (Halogens): 7 valence e-, highly reactive nonmetals

Group 18 (Noble Gases): full outer shell, very unreactive

Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It typically occurs between a metal and a nonmetal.

Formation Steps:

  1. Metal atom transfers one or more valence electrons to nonmetal atom.
  2. Metal becomes a positively charged cation.
  3. Nonmetal becomes a negatively charged anion.
  4. Electrostatic attraction between cation and anion forms the ionic bond.

Example: Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Na (1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹) loses 1e- → Na+ (1s²2s²2p⁶)

Cl (1s²2s²2p⁵3s²3p⁵) gains 1e- → Cl- (1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶)

Chemical Formulas & Valency

Chemical formulas indicate types of atoms and their quantities. Subscripts show atom counts (no subscript = 1).

Valency = magnitude of charge on most common ion. Metals = positive, Nonmetals = negative. Sum must equal zero in neutral compounds.

Example: MgCl2

Mg (Group 2) → Mg2+, valency 2

Cl (Group 17) → Cl-, valency 1

Balance: +2 + x×(-1) = 0 → x = 2

Formula: MgCl2

Pregnancy & Development

Gestation: ~40 weeks (280 days) from last menstrual period, ~38 weeks from fertilization.

Placenta: Develops from trophoblast and uterine tissue. Facilitates nutrient/gas exchange, removes waste, produces hormones.

Amniotic Sac & Fluid: Surrounds fetus, cushions, maintains temperature, allows movement.

Hormones

hCG: From developing placenta, signals corpus luteum to continue progesterone/estrogen production.

Progesterone: Maintains endometrium, prevents uterine contractions.

Estrogen: Supports uterus and mammary gland growth.

Developmental Stages

Embryonic Period (Weeks 1-8): Major organs and body systems form. Three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm) differentiate. Recognizable human form by end.

Fetal Period (Week 9 to Birth): Growth and maturation. Sex organs develop. Bones/muscles develop. Viable ~24 weeks with medical support.

Birth (Parturition): Uterine contractions, cervix dilation, expulsion of baby and placenta.

Cell Division: Mitosis vs Meiosis

Mitosis

Purpose: Growth, tissue repair, asexual reproduction.

Outcome: 2 genetically identical diploid (2n) daughter cells.

Phases:

Interphase: Cell grows, DNA replicates, prepares for division.

Prophase: Chromosomes condense, nuclear envelope breaks down.

Metaphase: Chromosomes align at metaphase plate (cell equator).

Anaphase: Sister chromatids separate, move to opposite poles.

Telophase: Chromosomes decondense, nuclear envelopes reform.

Cytokinesis: Cytoplasm divides, two daughter cells form.

Meiosis

Purpose: Production of gametes (sperm and eggs).

Outcome: 4 genetically distinct haploid (n) daughter cells.

Meiosis I (Reductional):

Prophase I: Homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis), crossing over occurs.

Metaphase I: Homologous pairs align at metaphase plate.

Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate. Sister chromatids stay attached.

Telophase I: Two haploid cells form.

Meiosis II (Equational):

Similar to mitosis. Sister chromatids separate. Result: 4 haploid cells.

Gametes & Fertilization

Gametes: Haploid (n) sex cells with half the chromosomes. Sperm (male, testes) and Egg/Ovum (female, ovaries).

Spermatogenesis: Produces millions of sperm daily.

Oogenesis: Produces one mature egg per menstrual cycle (plus non-viable polar bodies).

Fertilization: Sperm + Egg = diploid (2n) zygote. Each contributes 23 chromosomes = 46 total (23 pairs).

Genetic Diversity: Through crossing over and independent assortment during meiosis, plus combination of two parents' genetic material.

Menstrual Cycle

Key Hormones

FSH: Stimulates follicle growth
LH: Triggers ovulation & corpus luteum
Estrogen: Thickens endometrium, triggers LH surge
Progesterone: Maintains endometrium

Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs)

Bacterial

Chlamydia: Caused by Chlamydia trachomatis. Often asymptomatic, can lead to PID and infertility.
Gonorrhea: Caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Can cause PID, infertility; transmittable to newborns.
Syphilis: Caused by Treponema pallidum. Progresses through stages (primary, secondary, latent, tertiary). Serious long-term problems if untreated.

Viral

HPV: Some types cause genital warts, others lead to cervical/anal cancers. Vaccines available.
HSV (Herpes): Painful sores. Lifelong infection.
HIV: Attacks immune system, leads to AIDS if untreated. Managed with antiretroviral therapy.
Hepatitis B: Affects liver. Vaccine available.

Parasitic

Trichomoniasis: Caused by Trichomonas vaginalis. Often asymptomatic but can cause discharge and irritation.

Prevention

Abstinence

Consistent condom use

Limiting partners

Vaccination (HPV, Hep B)

Regular STD testing

Types of Muscles

The muscular system enables movement, maintains posture, produces heat (~85% of body heat), protects internal organs, and facilitates blood circulation.

Skeletal Muscle

Location: Attached to bones

Appearance: Striated (striped)

Control: Voluntary

Function: Locomotion, posture, heat generation

Cells: Long, cylindrical, multinucleated

Smooth Muscle

Location: Walls of internal organs

Appearance: Non-striated (smooth)

Control: Involuntary

Function: Propels substances, regulates blood vessel diameter

Cells: Spindle-shaped, single nucleus

Cardiac Muscle

Location: Wall of the heart

Appearance: Striated (with intercalated discs)

Control: Involuntary

Function: Pumps blood throughout the body

Cells: Branched, single nucleus, intercalated discs

Drugs in Sport

Doping is the use of banned substances or methods by athletes to improve performance. Performance-Enhancing Drugs (PEDs) are substances taken to improve athletic ability.

The Haitian Revolution

Explore the causes, key figures, and lasting impact of the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history (1791–1804).

Introduction

The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was the most successful slave revolt in history. It took place in Saint-Domingue, a French colony on the western third of the island of Hispaniola (modern-day Haiti). The colony was the wealthiest in the Caribbean, producing vast quantities of sugar, coffee, and indigo — all through the brutal exploitation of enslaved Africans.

By the late 18th century, Saint-Domingue had approximately 500,000 enslaved people, 32,000 white colonists, and 28,000 free people of colour (gens de couleur libres). The enslaved outnumbered free people by roughly 10 to 1.

The revolution resulted in the abolition of slavery and the establishment of Haiti as the first free Black republic on 1 January 1804, making it the second independent nation in the Western Hemisphere (after the United States).

Causes of the Revolution

Brutal Slavery System

Enslaved people endured horrific conditions on sugar plantations. The Code Noir regulated their treatment but allowed extreme punishments including branding, mutilation, and execution. Life expectancy on plantations was extremely short.

Enlightenment Ideas

The French Revolution (1789) and its ideals of "liberty, equality, fraternity" inspired both free people of colour and enslaved people. If all men were equal, slavery was a direct contradiction of these principles.

Social Tensions

Free people of colour (gens de couleur libres) were wealthy and educated but denied political rights. Grands Blancs and Petits Blancs competed for power. These divisions weakened the colony's ability to resist revolt.

Life of an Enslaved Person

The Middle Passage

Captured Africans were transported across the Atlantic in horrific conditions. Packed tightly in ships, many died from disease, dehydration, or violence. Survivors arrived traumatized and were sold at auction.

Plantation Work

Enslaved people worked 16–18 hour days on sugar, coffee, and indigo plantations. Sugar production was especially dangerous — workers suffered burns, lost limbs in mills, and were whipped for slowing down.

The Code Noir

This French law regulated slavery: enslaved people were legally property, could not own land, and had no legal rights. Punishments included branding with the fleur-de-lis, ear-cropping, and death for striking a master.

Resistance & Culture

Despite oppression, enslaved people maintained African traditions through Vodou (blending African spirituality with Catholicism), music, and oral history. Maroon communities of escaped slaves established independent settlements in the mountains.

Social Structure of Saint-Domingue

TOP

Grands Blancs (Big Whites)

Wealthy plantation owners, top colonial officials. They controlled the economy and political power, owned most of the land and enslaved people. Many lived lavishly.

MIDDLE

Petits Blancs (Small Whites)

Shopkeepers, artisans, overseers, and poor whites. They had fewer resources but still held legal privileges over all people of colour. Resented both the Grands Blancs above and free coloureds below.

RESTRICTED

Gens de Couleur Libres (Free People of Colour)

Mixed-race and free Black individuals. Many were educated and wealthy (some owned plantations and slaves themselves) but were denied political rights, couldn't hold public office, and faced social discrimination.

BOTTOM

Enslaved Africans

Approximately 500,000 people with no rights, treated as property. They performed all plantation labor and endured extreme violence. Despite everything, they maintained cultural identity and ultimately led the revolution.

Key Leaders

TL

Toussaint Louverture

c.1743–1803

A self-educated former slave who became the revolution's greatest military and political leader. He united different factions, negotiated with European powers, and drafted a constitution. Captured by the French through deception and died in a French prison in 1803.

JD

Jean-Jacques Dessalines

c.1758–1806

Born into slavery, Dessalines was a fierce military commander who took over after Louverture's capture. He defeated Napoleon's forces decisively and declared Haiti's independence on 1 January 1804, becoming its first ruler (Emperor Jacques I).

BD

Boukman Dutty

d.1791

A Vodou priest and enslaved man who led the Bois Caïman ceremony in August 1791 — a pivotal Vodou gathering that spiritually united the enslaved and sparked the initial uprising. He was killed early in the revolution but became a powerful symbol.

HC

Henri Christophe

1767–1820

A key military leader during the revolution. After independence, he ruled northern Haiti as King Henri I and built the famous Citadelle Laferrière, a massive mountaintop fortress — the largest in the Americas.

Timeline of Key Events

1789

French Revolution Begins

Ideas of liberty and equality spread to the colonies, inspiring both free coloureds and the enslaved.

1791

Bois Caïman Ceremony & Uprising

Boukman Dutty leads a Vodou ceremony that unites the enslaved. Within days, the northern plains erupt in revolt — plantations burn and thousands join the uprising.

1793

Slavery Abolished in Saint-Domingue

French commissioners abolish slavery locally to win Black support against Spanish and British invasions. Toussaint Louverture emerges as a dominant leader.

1801

Louverture's Constitution

Toussaint Louverture drafts a constitution making himself governor-for-life and abolishing slavery permanently. Napoleon sees this as a threat and sends 20,000 troops.

1802

Louverture Captured

Tricked into a meeting under false pretenses, Louverture is arrested and deported to France where he dies in prison in 1803. Dessalines takes command.

1804

Haiti Declares Independence

On 1 January 1804, Jean-Jacques Dessalines declares independence. Haiti becomes the first free Black republic and the second independent nation in the Western Hemisphere.
